Alphabet: the 2027 depreciation wave and a margin guardrail

Alphabet will spend about $200B on property and equipment in 2026, mostly AI servers and data centers. I built a data pipeline and a forecast model from SEC filings to show what that spend does to the 2027 P&L, and how fast other costs can grow if the operating margin is to hold.

Key data

Role
Solo, end to end
Data

Tools

The ask

How much will Alphabet's AI build-out raise 2027 depreciation, how much of it is already locked in, and how fast can other costs grow if the operating margin is to hold?

What I did

  • Pulled 269 facts from the SEC XBRL API: CapEx, depreciation, revenue and operating income, 2007 to Q3 2026.
  • Wrote a SQL pipeline in BigQuery dialect, run on DuckDB: the latest filing wins, quarters come from year-to-date figures, and five tie-out checks run against the filings.
  • Built a Python vintage model: each quarter's spend splits by asset type, waits in construction, then depreciates straight-line once it goes live.
  • Fit the model on data through 2024 only and backtested it on the next seven quarters before trusting the forecast.
  • Mirrored it in a 9-tab Excel plan with 1,045 live formulas, scenario switches and 15 checks, then wrote a CFO memo and a 9-slide deck.

What I found

My call

Cap 2027 growth in costs other than depreciation at 14%, versus 18.9% in 2026E. That holds the operating margin near 33% at 19% revenue growth.

Show locked-in depreciation next to CapEx in monthly reporting, so every CapEx approval shows what it adds to 2027 and 2028 depreciation before it is signed.

Re-test the plan after the Q4 2026 call. Triggers: 2027 CapEx guidance outside $250B to $350B, any useful-life change, or Q4 2026 depreciation outside $9.0B to $9.9B (model: $9.4B).

Limits: one blended life per asset class, construction timing is a fitted average rather than project-level data, and the server share rests on one CFO comment. Each 5 points of server share moves 2027E by about $1.2B.

Econometrics research · ECON 352 · Fall 2026

Do minimum wage hikes cost teen jobs?

No detectable job loss in the first year. Across 33 state increases of $0.25 or more, the estimated effect on teen employment is +0.11 percentage points, statistically indistinguishable from zero.

Key data

Role
Solo, end to end
Status
Final paper
Data
IPUMS CPS Basic Monthly, 2010-2019; Vaghul and Zipperer minimum wages
Method
Stacked DiD, event study, wild cluster bootstrap
Tools
Stata
Best fit
Data analyst, economic research, risk analytics
+0.11 ppEffect on teen employmentWild bootstrap p = 0.83, not significant
33Treated state-eventsIncreases of $0.25+, in 11 cohorts
10,008State-month observationsStacked; all 50 states and DC
p = 0.41Pre-trend testWild bootstrap; no sign of diverging trends

The ask

What is the causal effect of state minimum-wage increases of at least $0.25 on employment among teens ages 16 to 19?

What I did

  • Built a survey-weighted state-month panel of teen employment from IPUMS CPS, merged with monthly state minimum wages.
  • Defined substantial events: increases of $0.25 or more, with no other such increase 24 months before or 11 months after.
  • Ran a stacked difference-in-differences: one sub-experiment per cohort, clean controls and corrective weights.
  • Tested parallel trends with a monthly event study, from 12 months before to 11 months after.
  • Used a wild cluster bootstrap (9,999 draws), since there are only 51 state clusters.

What I found

Takeaway

No evidence that increases of $0.25 or more cut teen jobs within a year. The estimate is small and not significant, and the event study shows no downward drift after increases.

A null result is not proof of zero. Limits: short-run effects only, noisy CPS samples in small states, and parallel trends can't be observed directly.

Design

Employmentsct = αsc + λct + β(Treatedsc × Postct) + X′sctγ + εsct

β is the effect. Fixed effects absorb state-by-cohort levels and cohort-specific monthly shocks.

  • Tested it across 5 market regimes, from the dot-com bust to the 2022-23 rate hikes.
  • Combined value, quality, momentum and growth into one weighted model.

What I found

Takeaway

Growth pays up to a point, then investors overpay. Weighted 40% value, 30% quality, 20% momentum and 10% growth, returns rise almost steadily from the bottom decile to the top.
