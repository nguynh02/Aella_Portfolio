Independent research · Oct. 2026

Indego Bike Share: where the growth comes from

Ride-minutes, the revenue proxy, grew 18.9% year over year. About 59% of the gain came from stations opened in 2025-26 and demand is shifting to e-bikes, so the call is a small, reversible e-bike pilot before any broad rollout.

Key data

Role
Solo, end to end
Data
2.26M trips in 6 quarterly files, 337 stations
Window
Jan.-Sep. 2026 vs. Jan.-Sep. 2025
Tools
Python (standard library), Excel, PowerPoint
Output
2-slide executive brief, code and data
+18.9%Ride-minutes17.68M to 21.02M
+19.2%Trips1.03M to 1.23M
17.08 minAverage trip17.12 min in 2025, nearly flat
+8.0%Pre-2025 networkRide-minutes at established stations

The question

Treat Indego, Philadelphia's bike share, as a for-profit business in the middle of a multi-year growth push, with revenue driven by trip duration. How should leaders measure growth, is it too fast or too slow, and what should they do next?

Approach

  • Streamed 2.26M trip records from six quarterly files in plain Python, without loading whole files into memory, and parsed both date formats Indego uses.
  • Compared each quarter with the same quarter a year earlier to remove seasonality, using ride-minutes as the revenue proxy and trips as the volume check.
  • Split the gain by station launch cohort and by bike type, so network expansion is not mistaken for organic demand.
  • Flagged data quality instead of hiding it: invalid durations, trips over 180 minutes, missing stations and wrong-period dates.
  • Wrote it up as a two-slide executive brief, with every limitation stated on the slide.

Findings

Recommendation

Pilot e-bike capacity at 3-5 proven-demand stations for 8-12 weeks, compare them with similar unmodified stations, and scale only if incremental ride-minutes per bike-day and contribution margin clear the bar.

Too fast or too slow? The public data cannot answer that yet, because pricing, fleet capacity and unit costs are missing. That is why the first step is a reversible pilot, not full capital spending.

Quantitative research · Summer 2025

A volatility-conditioned RSI strategy on TSLA

A swing-trading rule that buys oversold turns only when volatility is falling, then exits on volatility-scaled targets. Over five years of daily TSLA data, the best version returned 34.6% a year with a 1.29 Sharpe ratio and a 12.2% maximum drawdown.

Key data

Role
Co-author
Authors
Tri Bui, Aella Nguyen
Faculty
Prof. Liang Ding
Data
TSLA daily prices, Jan. 2020 - Jan. 2025
Output
14-page research paper
34.6%CAGR21.9% with a trailing stop
1.29Sharpe ratio0.80 with a trailing stop
12.2%Maximum drawdown22.3% with a trailing stop
8,320Parameter setsGrid search with a drawdown penalty

The question

Can a simple reversal signal hold up on one of the most volatile large-cap stocks if risk control comes first?

Approach

  • Required three signals to agree before buying: RSI below its oversold threshold, RSI turning up, and volatility falling.
  • Exited with volatility-scaled rules, an ATR-based take-profit and stop-loss, and tested them against a trailing-stop exit.
  • Searched 8,320 parameter sets across RSI periods of 5-35 days, volatility windows of 5-25 days and thresholds of 20-45 and 65-80, penalizing drawdowns above 25%.
  • Judged each version on Sharpe, Sortino, CAGR, maximum drawdown, profit factor, win rate and holding period.

Findings

Takeaway

The ATR exit beats the trailing stop on every risk measure, with about half the maximum drawdown. Its win rate is only 41%, so the edge comes from letting winners run: gross profits are 1.66 times gross losses.

Factor research · Team project

Does sales growth predict stock returns?

A 27-year backtest, Dec. 1996 to Dec. 2023, of decile portfolios sorted on trailing 12-month sales growth. The fastest growers delivered the worst returns, so the team built a four-factor model that turns the long-short spread from −5.5% to +6.7%.

Key data

Role
Co-author, team of 4
Window
Dec. 1996 - Dec. 2023
Method
Decile sorts, monthly rebalancing, long-short spread
Output
16-slide presentation
−6.12%Top-growth decileWorst average return of all ten
+2.63%4th decileBest average return
+6.7%Model long-shortvs. −5.5% for growth alone
53.8%Model hit ratevs. 47.9% for growth alone

The question

Investors pay up for fast revenue growth. Does that growth show up in future stock returns, and does it belong in a multi-factor model?

Approach

  • Profiled the factor first: median trailing sales growth of 8.2%, mean of 13.0%, strong right skew (1.68) and fat tails (kurtosis 4.44).
  • Backtested ten decile portfolios with monthly rebalancing, plus a long-short spread of the top decile minus the bottom.
  • Broke results out by regime: the dot-com bust, the financial crisis, the 2013-2018 expansion, the 2020-21 stimulus boom and the 2022-23 rate hikes.
  • Measured rank correlation with value, quality and momentum, then combined all four into a model weighted 40% value, 30% quality, 20% momentum and 10% growth.

Findings

Takeaway

Growth pays only up to a point: returns peak in the 4th decile and collapse at the top, where the hit rate falls to 44.4%. The factor works in booms with cheap money and fails in crises and rate shocks. Inside the combined model, returns rise almost steadily from the bottom decile to the top.

Econometrics research · ECON 352 Advanced Econometrics · Fall 2026 In progress

Do minimum wage increases cost teen jobs?

A causal study of how state minimum-wage increases affect employment among 16 to 19 year olds, built on 535,323 person-month observations from the Current Population Survey and a staggered difference-in-differences design.

Key data

Role
Solo
Status
Data and design complete; estimation next
Data
IPUMS CPS Basic Monthly, 2010-2019; state minimum wages (Vaghul and Zipperer, 2022)
Method
Staggered DiD (Callaway and Sant'Anna, 2021), event study
535,323Person-month observationsCivilian teens, ages 16-19
36StatesAll at $7.25 in Jan. 2010
15 / 21Treated / comparisonRaised the wage vs. stayed at $7.25
120MonthsJan. 2010 - Dec. 2019

The question

Theory disagrees. Competitive labor markets predict job losses when the wage floor rises, while monopsony models predict small or no losses. Among states at the $7.25 federal floor in Jan. 2010, what did later increases do to teen employment?

Approach

  • Merged individual CPS records with monthly state minimum wages by state, year and month; every observation matched.
  • Tightened the design after the proposal: only states at $7.25 in Jan. 2010 enter, each is treated from its first full month above the federal floor, and states that never move form the comparison group.
  • Built survey-weighted descriptive statistics with the CPS final basic weight (WTFINL).
  • Specified a staggered difference-in-differences model with state and month fixed effects, an event-study test of pre-trends and state-clustered standard errors.

Design

Baseline model, with state and month fixed effects:

Employmentist = αs + λt + β·Treatmentst + X′istγ + εist

The key assumption is parallel trends. Treated states have lower teen employment throughout (27.8% vs. 30.5%), which is fine; what matters is whether their trends would have matched without the increase. The event study tests this before treatment begins.

Citi: Q4 2022 operating plan

Rebuilt Citi's Q4 2022 operating plan in a finance job simulation: a P&L walk from segment revenue to net income, a study of how COVID-19 waves moved card spending, and a deposit-limits report that caught a trigger breach.

Key data

The question

How do higher rates, credit costs and consumer behavior flow through Citi's P&L, and where are the early warning signs?

Approach

  • Walked the P&L across four segments, from $18.0B of revenue (+6% on higher rates) to $2.5B of net income (−21%).
  • Traced the decline to credit: a $640M reserve build and 36% higher net credit losses lifted cost of credit to $1.8B, after a $0.5B net release a year earlier.
  • Charted card spending in staples, work-related, delayable and social categories against COVID-19 cases from Jan. 2020 to Dec. 2021 to project Omicron's impact.
  • Tracked deposit surpluses against management action triggers and flagged Surplus 1 falling below zero in late February.

Findings

Takeaway

Revenue grew, but cost of credit swung by $2.3B, from a $0.5B release to a $1.8B charge, enough to cut net income 21%. On the consumer side, the link between COVID-19 waves and card spending weakened over time: Omicron brought a mild total dip, steady staples and sharp drops in social and work-related spending.
